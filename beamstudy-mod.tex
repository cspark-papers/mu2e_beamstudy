\begin{abstract}
Spill regulation in resonant extraction depends on the beam response to transverse excitation and on the time required for unstable particles to reach the septum. We study these effects for the Fermilab Mu2e Delivery Ring using Synergia2 tracking and a reduced Kobayashi-Hamiltonian model, with MARS transport to examine the subsequent motion of septum-scattered particles. The tested radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) controller reduces visible ripple-driven spill modulation, with residual spikes. Separate fixed-tune tests show greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions at nonzero chromaticity, consistent with chromatic phase mixing. Tune-step diagnostics at three extraction stages show long arrival delays near unstable fixed points; the reduced model captures the main trends but differs from full tracking near the separatrix. In the stated $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil scattering model, approximately 43\% of the selected sample returned from MARS is lost during subsequent ring tracking. This fraction is conditional on that sample and is not a total extraction-loss estimate. These results extend the published feed-forward study by identifying particle-response effects relevant to RFKO regulation and tracing transport beyond septum interception. The calculations support configuration-specific interpretations of these mechanisms; they do not quantify the performance of a complete spill-control system.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}

Slow resonant extraction from the Delivery Ring is part of the proton-delivery scheme for the Mu2e experiment at Fermilab~\cite{tdr}. Harmonic sextupoles excite the third-integer resonance, and a ramp of the tune-control quadrupoles moves the beam toward resonance. The preceding Synergia2 study~\cite{mu2e} established a feed-forward approach based on a piecewise linear tune ramp, septum-plane alignment, and dynamic local-orbit bumps. It treated the extraction geometry and average spill evolution, while leaving radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) feedback outside its simulation scope. The present study examines the particle response relevant to RFKO regulation and the subsequent transport of particles scattered at the septum.

The extraction geometry provides the context for both questions. The Hardt condition aligns outgoing separatrices for different momenta and reduces the momentum dependence of the extracted-beam angle~\cite{hardt,pullia,pimms}. In the dispersion-free extraction straight, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} used four time-dependent correctors to control the septum entrance angle. That geometric optimization forms the published baseline for the present particle-response studies.

For its optimized tune ramp, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} reported an open-loop spill within approximately $\pm20\%$ of the mean. RFKO supplies transverse excitation whose amplitude can be adjusted in response to the spill signal~\cite{HB10,ipac11}. Interpreting that response requires distinguishing coherent motion of the bunch from growth of its internal phase-space area, and accounting for the finite time between destabilization and arrival at the septum. We address these aspects through a feedback test under imposed quadrupole ripple, fixed-tune RFKO tests at two chromaticities, and tune-step diagnostics at three stages of extraction.

Septum scattering raises a complementary transport question: where do particles travel or undergo subsequent loss after intercepting extraction material? The published aperture model removes particles classified as septum-head hits or foil-plane crossings~\cite{mu2e}. Here, MARS transports intercepted particles through the extraction channel, and Synergia2 follows a selected returned sample around the ring~\cite{mars1,mars2}. This calculation extends the description beyond geometric interception to the subsequent motion and loss locations of the scattered sample.

The three models serve distinct purposes. Synergia2 provides multiparticle tracking with space charge~\cite{synergia2}; the reduced Kobayashi Hamiltonian isolates horizontal resonance dynamics; and MARS describes interactions with material. Their combined use connects the observed spill and phase-space behavior to particle motion, while the configuration and population boundaries in Sec.~\ref{sec:scope} define the extent of the comparison. The contribution relative to Ref.~\cite{mu2e} is the set of feedback, transit-time, and post-interception diagnostics, building on established RFKO and resonance descriptions.

Section~\ref{sec:scope} relates the calculations to the published baseline and defines the individual simulation cases. Section~\ref{sec:rfko1} presents the feedback response, and Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking} develops the reduced model and examines transit times and scattered-particle transport. Section~\ref{sec:rfko} addresses chromaticity-dependent RFKO excitation, and Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion} brings together the implications for spill regulation and loss interpretation.

\subsection{\label{sec:cases}Simulation cases}

The calculations comprise the five cases in Table~\ref{table:cases}. Each is interpreted individually. Together they provide diagnostics of feedback response, transit dynamics, and scattering transport, not a common, validated model of integrated spill regulation and extraction efficiency. The model comparisons identify qualitative mechanisms without isolating every source of disagreement or establishing numerical controller performance.

Several intervals of about 100 turns occur in this paper and describe different operations. In Ref.~\cite{mu2e}, 100 turns is the sextupole turn-on period and the width of the moving window used to display the spill. In the feedback case, the spill signal is accumulated over approximately 100 turns, about $0.17\,\mathrm{ms}$, and each RFKO chirp sweep lasts 100 turns. Neither interval specifies the controller update interval, which is not documented. Similarly, the 200-turn segments of the published feed-forward ramp are unrelated to the approximately 300-turn pause and 500-turn hold that define the tune-step diagnostic.

\section{\label{sec:rfko1}Spill regulation with RFKO feedback}

The feed-forward result of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} motivates regulating the fluctuations that remain after the average spill has been flattened. The present feedback test adds an imposed quadrupole ripple and a transverse RFKO kick near the horizontal betatron frequency. It examines the beam response to feedback in the stated simulation case, rather than re-establishing the published tune-ramp and dynamic-bump optimization.

The proposed feedback loop comprises an RFKO kicker, a spill monitor, and a controller. A wall-current monitor in the external beamline supplies the extracted-beam signal used to adjust the kicker amplitude~\cite{wcm}. The simulated control scheme uses a spill signal accumulated over approximately 100 turns, corresponding to about $0.17\,\mathrm{ms}$. This averaging interval and the particle transit-time distribution both contribute to the effective response time. Section~\ref{sec:cases} distinguishes it from other intervals of similar length.

The RFKO center frequency follows the betatron frequency associated with the ramped machine tune. Frequency modulation broadens the excitation to cover the chromatic tune spread. In the extraction simulations, the horizontal chromaticity is set to $C_x=d\nu_x/d\delta=1.0$, where $\delta=(p-p_0)/p_0$, using chromaticity-correction sextupoles. The incoherent space-charge tune spread alone need not provide the phase mixing required for efficient emittance growth under dipole excitation~\cite{ipac11}; Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko} examines this mechanism at fixed tune.

The excitation uses a linear frequency chirp with a sweep duration of 100 turns. Repeating an identical sweep produces discrete spectral lines. Randomizing the phase at the start of each sweep reduces this periodic structure and broadens the excitation spectrum.

To test the response to a controlled disturbance, a ripple at $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is applied to the quadrupole strengths. The original simulation description gives its size as a ``$\pm0.5\%$ peak-to-peak'' variation of the nominal strengths, which does not establish whether 0.5\% is the amplitude or the full peak-to-peak excursion; the waveform is not specified. The ripple produces pronounced, quasi-periodic spill modulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig9}, middle). Its dominant visible modulation repeats several times within one $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ period, which corresponds to about 9,830 turns, so the relation between the applied waveform and the spill spectrum is not established by the displayed traces.

Controllers based on proportional--integral--derivative feedback were investigated~\cite{pid}. Proportional-only control produced large spill oscillations (not shown), consistent with overcorrection when the beam responds to the kicker with a delay. In the original study, a proportional gain that varied during the spill, combined with a constant derivative gain, performed best among the tested configurations; the comparison criterion and gain values are not documented. The result shown here therefore uses proportional--derivative control, without an integral term.

Figure~\ref{fig:fig9} compares the three configurations. Each trace begins with a brief start-up transient. Without ripple or feedback (top), the normalized spill fluctuates about unity with small, fast variations. The imposed ripple (middle) adds larger, quasi-periodic excursions with a sawtooth-like shape. With feedback (bottom), this modulation is visibly smaller, although still discernible, and isolated spikes appear; the largest of these slightly exceed the largest excursions without feedback.

Both features are consistent with a loop whose corrections reach the spill only after a delay. The controller acts on a spill averaged over about 100 turns, and particles moved toward the separatrix by the kicker reach the septum only after transit times that range from a few turns to more than one hundred turns in the tune-step diagnostic (Sec.~\ref{sec:arrival}). A correction therefore responds to spill changes that have already occurred: slow modulation can be reduced, whereas abrupt changes can be under- or overcorrected. The derivative term, which responds to the rate of change of the spill, is a standard means of damping the oscillation seen with proportional-only control~\cite{pid}. This interpretation is qualitative; the traces do not establish a numerical suppression factor, a stability margin, or an optimum set of gains.

Within these limits, the result shows that, in this space-charge tracking model, an RFKO loop acting on a spill averaged over about 100 turns reduces the visible effect of an imposed quadrupole ripple. The factor of 1.5--3 ripple reduction discussed in Ref.~\cite{mu2e} was an expectation based on other facilities, not a Mu2e closed-loop result, and the present traces do not test that number. An integral term could reduce steady-state error, but its effect on stability and spill fluctuations has not been examined here.

\section{\label{sec:tracking}Particle tracking simulations}

\subsection{\label{sec:model}Reduced resonance model and Kobayashi Hamiltonian}

Let $n$ be the turn number and $\theta=2\pi n$ the corresponding orbital angle, both treated as continuous. Let $x$ be the horizontal displacement from the reference orbit, $x'=dx/ds$ its slope with respect to the path length $s$, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the horizontal Twiss parameters at the observation point. The normalized coordinates are
\begin{equation}
 X=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 X'=\frac{\alpha x+\beta x'}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 a=\frac{X+iX'}{\sqrt{2}}.
 \label{eqn:coordinates}
\end{equation}
Here $X'$ is the momentum conjugate to $X$, not $dX/ds$. When $x$ and $\beta$ are expressed in consistent length units, $X$ and $X'$ have units of $\sqrt{\mathrm{length}}$. The geometric Courant--Snyder action,
\begin{equation}
 I_x=a^*a=\frac{X^2+X'^2}{2}
     =\frac{x^2+(\alpha x+\beta x')^2}{2\beta},
 \label{eqn:action}
\end{equation}
has units of length, for example $\mathrm{mm\,mrad}$, and is consistent with the invariant used for the phase-space aperture in Eq.~(6) of Ref.~\cite{mu2e}.

Action and emittance are related but distinct. For a matched distribution centered on the reference orbit, the rms geometric emittance equals the mean action $\langle I_x\rangle$. The normalized emittance is larger by the relativistic factor $p/(m_pc)\simeq9.47$ for the momentum in Table~\ref{table:param}. For a Gaussian distribution, 95\% of the particles satisfy $2I_x\le6\langle I_x\rangle$, which is a common basis for quoting a 95\% emittance. If the centroid oscillates, an emittance computed about the centroid excludes the coherent motion, whereas the mean action about the reference orbit includes it. This distinction underlies the comparison of emittance growth and centroid motion in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}.

In a frame rotating with the resonant tune $29/3$, so that a particle exactly on resonance is stationary when observed once per turn, and with $\theta$ as the independent variable, the leading-order third-integer Hamiltonian is~\cite{preliminary}
\begin{equation}
 H=\Delta\nu\,a^*a-ig a^3+ig^*(a^*)^3+\cdots.
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian1}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu_x$ is the horizontal betatron tune, $\Delta\nu=\nu_x-29/3$ is its distance from resonance, and $g$ is the complex resonance-driving coefficient, with units of $\mathrm{length}^{-1/2}$, determined by the harmonic-sextupole strengths and phases. The two cubic terms are complex conjugates, so $H$ is real; higher-order terms are neglected. The equations of motion are $da/d\theta=-i\,\partial H/\partial a^*$, equivalent to $dX/d\theta=\partial H/\partial X'$ and $dX'/d\theta=-\partial H/\partial X$.

The phase of the resonance coefficient must be compared using the same coordinate convention. With $a=\sqrt{I_x}e^{-i\phi_x}$, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:hamiltonian1} has the angle--action form of Eq.~(1) in Ref.~\cite{mu2e} when its coefficient is written as $g_{\mathrm{aa}}=ig$. This phase factor relates the two Hamiltonian expressions; it does not establish the phase convention used in the original tracking implementation.

Writing $g=|g|e^{i\Psi}$ and substituting Eq.~\eqref{eqn:coordinates} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 H={}&\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}(X^2+X'^2)\\
 &+\frac{|g|}{\sqrt{2}}\left[(X^3-3XX'^2)\sin\Psi
             +(3X^2X'-X'^3)\cos\Psi\right].
\end{split}
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian2}
\end{equation}
The phase $\Psi$ sets the orientation of the separatrix; we use $\Psi=\pi$, for which the $\sin\Psi$ term vanishes. Measuring time in units of three turns with $\tau=n/3=\theta/(6\pi)$ multiplies $H$ by $6\pi$ and gives the Kobayashi Hamiltonian~\cite{kobayashi},
\begin{equation}
 H_K=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(X^2+X'^2)
       +\frac{S}{4}(3X^2X'-X'^3),
 \label{eqn:kobayashi}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon=6\pi\Delta\nu$ and $S=-12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|$. The symbol $\epsilon$ follows Kobayashi's notation for the tune distance and is not an emittance. The negative sign of $S$ follows from $\Psi=\pi$. For the below-resonance setting $\Delta\nu<0$ considered here, it agrees with the expression $12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|\,\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta\nu)$, which does not define a fixed separatrix orientation in general.

The quadratic term describes rotation in normalized phase space, counterclockwise for $\Delta\nu<0$ in the present convention. The cubic term bounds a triangular stable region around the stable fixed point at the origin. Below resonance, $\epsilon/S=|\Delta\nu|/(2\sqrt{2}|g|)>0$, and the three unstable fixed points are
\begin{equation}
 (X,X')=\left(0,\ \frac{4\epsilon}{3S}\right),\qquad
 \left(\pm\frac{2\epsilon}{\sqrt{3}\,S},\ -\frac{2\epsilon}{3S}\right),
 \label{eqn:fixedpoints}
\end{equation}
all at distance $4\epsilon/(3S)$ from the origin. The separatrix consists of the three straight lines $X'=-2\epsilon/(3S)$ and $X'=4\epsilon/(3S)\pm\sqrt{3}X$, which join these hyperbolic points. Their extensions beyond the vertices are the branches along which unstable particles leave the stable region and move toward the septum. The linear size of the stable region is proportional to $|\Delta\nu|$, so the region shrinks as the tune approaches resonance. The leftmost unstable fixed point, marked in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival2}, has $X=-2\epsilon/(\sqrt{3}S)$.

In terms of $\tau$, Hamilton's equations are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
  \frac{dX}{d\tau} & = \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X^{\prime}}
                  = \epsilon X^{\prime} + \frac{3}{4} S
                    \left(
                      X^{2} - X^{\prime 2}
                    \right), \\
  \frac{dX^{\prime}}{d\tau} & = - \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X}
                           = - \epsilon X - \frac{3}{2} S X X^{\prime}.
 \end{split}
\label{eqn:motion}
\end{equation}
One unit of $\tau$ corresponds to three turns, so the elapsed time is $t=nT_{\mathrm{rev}}=3T_{\mathrm{rev}}\tau$. The momentum and circumference in Table~\ref{table:param} give $T_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq1.69\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, consistent with $54\,\mathrm{ms}$ over approximately 31,795 turns. The three-turn interval is short compared with the spill duration, but this separation of timescales alone does not guarantee adiabatic motion near a separatrix. The tune steps used below are explicitly nonadiabatic changes followed by tracking at fixed quadrupole strengths.

The reduced model retains horizontal resonance dynamics but omits space charge and detailed lattice and extraction-channel effects. It is used to interpret the Synergia2 trajectories and their transit times. Agreement in qualitative features does not by itself validate the full tracking or scattering calculation.


\subsection{\label{sec:arrival}Transit-time distributions}

The transit time of a particle is the interval between its becoming unstable and its reaching the septum field region~\cite{pullia}. This single-particle quantity has no common time origin. The diagnostic used here instead measures, for each selected particle, the elapsed time from a common tune step, namely the restoration of the quadrupole strengths described below, until the particle reaches the septum field region. According to the original simulation description, transit times are recorded for the particles in the narrow phase-space shell released by the step. For these particles the two definitions nearly coincide, because the step makes them unstable. The criterion used to select them is not documented, so particles already in transit before the step may also contribute, and their elapsed times are shorter than their full transit times. The numerical arrival criterion is not documented for either calculation.

Analytical treatments based on the Kobayashi Hamiltonian describe transit times for fixed or slowly varying resonance conditions~\cite{pullia}. Near an unstable fixed point, the phase-space velocity becomes small and escape can take many turns. This produces a strong dependence on initial conditions and long transit-time tails. Such behavior reflects slow passage near a hyperbolic point, located by Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fixedpoints}, rather than trapping by a stable fixed point.

The Synergia2 calculation starts with $5\times10^5$ macroparticles, the parameters in Table~\ref{table:param}, and a tune-quadrupole ramp described as optimized for a uniform spill. At a selected turn, the ramp is paused for approximately 300 turns, or about 1\% of the spill duration. The strengths are then stepped to the values that the uninterrupted ramp would have reached at that turn and held for a further observation interval of approximately 500 turns. The step reduces the stable region and releases a population near its boundary. Whether the uninterrupted reference ramp is identical to the published 200-turn-segment ramp of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} is not documented. The procedure probes the extraction response to a finite tune change; it is not a measurement of the feedback-loop transfer function.

The pauses begin at turns 2,000, 15,000, and 25,000, representing early, middle, and late extraction. As the ramp proceeds, the stable region shrinks, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fixedpoints}, and the distance from the unstable fixed points to the septum grows. The three stages share one simulated beam, which allows these trends to be compared, although the number of particles contributing to each distribution differs and no statistical-convergence study is presented.

Figure~\ref{fig:arrival1a} shows the evolution of the circulating-beam fraction. The uninterrupted ramp produces a nearly steady decrease in beam intensity. Pausing the ramp slows extraction, and the subsequent tune step produces an additional decrease in circulating intensity. The corresponding normalized spill traces in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival1b} show a pronounced burst in the early-stage case and a weaker, broader response at the later stages. The burst size follows how strongly the pause suppresses extraction: at the early stage the spill nearly stops during the pause, so the step releases a larger accumulated population, whereas at the later stages extraction continues at a reduced rate. The original simulation description attributes this difference to a thicker phase-space shell released at the early stage. In each case the burst follows the restoration line after a visible delay and spreads over a finite interval, as expected from the distribution of transit times of the released particles in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival2}. After the burst, the paused-and-restored case extracts more slowly than the uninterrupted ramp for the rest of the displayed interval: its spill stays below, and its circulating fraction above, the corresponding blue trace. This difference is expected while the quadrupole strengths are held fixed and the uninterrupted ramp continues. The displayed interval extends beyond the approximately 500-turn hold, and the ramp protocol after the hold is not documented. Continued extraction during a pause is consistent with particles already traveling toward the septum and, in the full calculation, with evolving collective dynamics. The traces alone do not isolate the contributions of these effects.

For comparison, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:motion} is integrated with a fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) method. The reduced-model trajectories use the same initial particle coordinates as the Synergia2 sample, and the effective resonance parameters are evaluated at the restored quadrupole settings. Space charge is omitted in the reduced model. The continuous variable $\tau$ must be converted to turns when comparing transit times; observing trajectories every three turns does not prescribe the numerical integration step.

Figure~\ref{fig:arrival2} compares individual-particle transit times and their marginal distributions. Both calculations give short and nearly identical transit times for particles starting between the septum and the fixed-point line, which are already on the outgoing branch. Transit times increase as the starting coordinate moves from the septum toward $x=0$. The longest times belong to particles starting on the far side of the fixed-point line; in the reduced model, such particles must travel along the separatrix past an unstable fixed point before reaching the outgoing branch. Away from the short-transit region, the Synergia2 times scatter more widely at a given starting coordinate and are generally longer than the reduced-model times. At the middle and late stages, however, the reduced-model points reach the longest times shown, for particles starting nearest $x=0$. The two calculations therefore differ systematically, but neither has uniformly the broader distribution. In the short-transit region, the transit times of both calculations fall on discrete levels, as expected because a particle on an outgoing branch returns to the septum side of phase space only once every three turns. From the early to the late stage, the fixed-point line moves toward $x=0$ while the septum line stays fixed, the starting coordinates concentrate closer to $x=0$, and the longest transit times shown are longer at the middle and late stages than at the early stage.

Transit time depends on the full initial phase-space coordinates and the resonance parameters, and in particular on how closely a trajectory passes an unstable fixed point, where the phase-space velocity vanishes. This slow passage accounts qualitatively for the long transit times in both calculations and for their increase at later stages, when the stable region is smaller and its unstable fixed points lie farther from the septum. Space charge can change individual trajectories and the effective resonance conditions in Synergia2. The comparison, however, also changes the lattice description and other model details, so the observed differences cannot be attributed uniquely to space charge. Because the hold lasts about 500 turns and the plotted range ends at 250 turns, the displayed distributions may also omit the longest delays.

For spill regulation, the relevant feature is the spread of arrival times: particles released at the same moment reach the septum over an interval ranging from a few turns to more than one hundred turns, and this interval lengthens at later stages. A change in tune or in RFKO amplitude therefore alters the spill gradually rather than at once, which contributes to the delayed response discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}. The diagnostic does not determine a delay constant or a transfer function, because the released population, its selection, and the observation window are specific to this procedure.


\subsection{\label{sec:beamloss}Tracking of particles scattered at the septum}

Particles moving along an outgoing separatrix branch enter the electrostatic septum field and receive a deflection toward the extraction line. The scattering model reported here contains two electrostatic septa with $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ foils. This differs from the $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ geometry in Table~1 and Sec.~5 of Ref.~\cite{mu2e}. Finite foil thickness and extent allow particles to intercept material and scatter. The published geometric hit/cross classification supplies the motivation for following those particles, but its optimized loss fraction is not an input normalization for the present scattering sample.

In the aperture treatment used here, a particle that intercepts a septum component is removed from the primary tracking sample. This does not determine its subsequent fate. A scattered particle may return to the field-free region, receive a partial electrostatic kick, be extracted, or be lost at another aperture. MARS transport is used to resolve these alternatives through the extraction-channel geometry before selected scattered particles are returned to ring tracking.

Although the initial scattered-particle distribution is approximately aligned with the septum, its subsequent distributions are curved. After three turns, the added circulating-particle group follows the outgoing separatrix branch, whereas scattered particles can lie away from that branch. \section{\label{sec:rfko}Effects of RFKO excitation on emittance and centroid motion}

  \centering
  \subfigure[Horizontal emittance]{

    \label{fig:rfko1a}}

Nonzero chromaticity produces greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions in these simulations.}
\end{figure}

The tune-quadrupole ramp drives the principal extraction process, while RFKO regulates the spill by changing the transverse amplitudes of particles near the stable-region boundary. To isolate how RFKO changes the beam distribution, this section considers excitation at a fixed machine tune without the third-integer resonance drive. The diagnostics are emittance, centroid motion, and the distribution of horizontal action.

A transverse dipole kick deflects every particle in the bunch by the same angle. If all particles had the same betatron tune, successive kicks would change only the coherent oscillation of the centroid, leaving the distribution about the centroid, and hence the emittance about the centroid, unchanged. A spread of betatron tunes makes the particles dephase after each kick, so the coherent oscillation filaments into a larger internal phase-space area: the centroid motion decays while the emittance grows. With chromaticity, the tune spread is $C_x\delta$, which is of order $10^{-3}$ for $C_x=1$ and the momentum spread in Table~\ref{table:param}. Earlier simulations found effective excitation when the RFKO center frequency follows the machine tune and the modulation bandwidth is comparable to the chromatic tune spread, and they found that the incoherent space-charge tune spread alone does not provide this mixing~\cite{ipac11}. A rigidly displaced bunch carries its direct space-charge field with it, so this field does not by itself change the frequency of the dipole oscillation.

Unlike the extraction baseline in Ref.~\cite{mu2e}, the horizontal machine tune is fixed at 9.65, and the resonance drive and tune ramp are disabled. The model includes space charge. A circular transverse aperture, specified as a $20\pi\,\mathrm{mm\,mrad}$ acceptance whose normalization is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}, removes particles outside the accepted region. A linear chirp with randomized sweep phases supplies the RFKO excitation; its spectrum is described as covering the betatron tune spread of the beam. Two horizontal chromaticities, $C_x=0$ and $C_x=1$, are compared. Because the chirp is described as covering the betatron tune spread, which is much smaller at $C_x=0$, the two cases may differ in excitation spectrum as well as in chromaticity; the numerical bandwidths are not documented. The assumed RFKO power is $500\,\mathrm{W}$, corresponding in the modeled kicker to an approximately $1\,\mu\mathrm{rad}$ horizontal kick.

Figure~\ref{fig:rfko1} shows the expected contrast. At $C_x=0$ the emittance stays nearly constant (Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1a}), while the centroid wanders irregularly with excursions of several millimeters and milliradians (Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1b}). This is the behavior of a beam with little tune spread: each randomized sweep adds a response of random phase to the coherent oscillation, whose amplitude therefore wanders rather than growing steadily. At $C_x=1$ the centroid remains near the origin while the emittance grows substantially, consistent with chromatic phase mixing converting the driven motion into internal spread. The growth slows during the later part of the run; because particles outside the aperture are removed, this slowing may partly reflect particle removal rather than a change in the excitation. Whether the plotted emittance is an rms or 95\% value, and whether it is computed about the centroid (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}), is not documented, so the comparison is qualitative. The small emittance change at $C_x=0$ does not mean that the RFKO force is absent: the excitation acts on every particle but, without a tune spread, mainly moves the bunch as a whole.

Figure~\ref{fig:rfko2} shows the diagnostic $f(\sqrt{I_x})/\sqrt{I_x}$, with $I_x$ the action of Eq.~\eqref{eqn:action}. If $f$ is the particle density in $\sqrt{I_x}$, this quantity is proportional to the particle density in normalized phase space at amplitude $\sqrt{2I_x}$, because the area of an annulus in the $(X,X')$ plane is proportional to $dI_x=2\sqrt{I_x}\,d\sqrt{I_x}$. For a Gaussian beam it is therefore a Gaussian function of $\sqrt{I_x}$. At $C_x=0$ the profiles at all times nearly coincide, consistent with the constant emittance in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1a}. At $C_x=1$ the central density falls steadily while the profile flattens and extends to larger amplitudes, beyond the plotted range. Phase-space density thus moves from the core toward large amplitudes, as expected when the excitation increases individual amplitudes in a diffusion-like manner. Because the aperture removes particles, the population is not conserved. Quantitative interpretation, including comparison with the acceptances in Table~\ref{table:scope}, would require the surviving population, the diagnostic units and normalization, and whether the action is measured about the reference orbit or the centroid.

For spill regulation, the relevant effect is the growth of individual amplitudes near the stable-region boundary, which governs how quickly particles cross the separatrix. The fixed-tune results show that nonzero chromaticity converts RFKO excitation into such growth, whereas at zero chromaticity the excitation mainly drives the bunch as a whole. This supports the use of $C_x=1$ in the feedback case of Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}. Because the resonance drive is disabled in this test, the results do not show how the increased spread translates into an extraction rate or quantify the resulting spill.


\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusions}

The calculations extend the published feed-forward extraction study for Mu2e~\cite{mu2e} with particle-response diagnostics relevant to RFKO spill regulation and transport beyond septum interception. In the feedback test, proportional--derivative control reduces visible ripple-driven spill modulation, although residual spikes remain. The separate fixed-tune tests show greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions at nonzero chromaticity. Together, these observations support distinguishing the excitation of coherent motion from the phase mixing that can increase the internal beam spread; the fixed-tune tests themselves do not measure extraction.

The tune-step diagnostics show a broad range of arrival times and long delays for particles near unstable fixed points. The reduced Kobayashi model captures the main trends, supporting slow escape near the separatrix as a qualitative explanation of delayed arrival. Its differences from full tracking are not uniquely attributable to space charge because the models also differ in their lattice description and other details. These results show why particle transit times are relevant to interpreting a delayed spill response.

The scattering study follows a selected population beyond the geometric interception model. Approximately 43\% of the sample returned from MARS is reported lost in subsequent ring tracking for the stated $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil configuration. This conditional fraction is distinct from the injected-beam loss quoted for the published $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil model. The existing loss map identifies where the returned particles are lost, while reduced-model trajectories explain their phase-space shearing qualitatively.

The evidence remains specific to the stated cases: it does not provide a quantitative suppression factor, validate an integrated feedback system, or determine total extraction efficiency. Within that scope, the results connect RFKO regulation to two complementary aspects of beam response---chromatic phase mixing and finite transit times---and distinguish primary septum interception from subsequent scattered-particle transport. This separation gives a physical basis for interpreting the spill and loss diagnostics presented here.
