\begin{abstract}
Spill regulation in resonant extraction depends on the beam response to transverse excitation and on the time required for unstable particles to reach the septum. We study these effects for the Fermilab Mu2e Delivery Ring using Synergia2 tracking and a reduced Kobayashi-Hamiltonian model, with MARS transport to examine the subsequent motion of septum-scattered particles. The tested radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) controller reduces visible ripple-driven spill modulation, with residual spikes. Separate fixed-tune tests show greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions at nonzero chromaticity, consistent with chromatic phase mixing. Tune-step diagnostics at three extraction stages show long arrival delays near unstable fixed points; the reduced model captures the main trends but differs from full tracking near the separatrix. In the stated $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil scattering model, approximately 43\% of the selected sample returned from MARS is lost during subsequent ring tracking. This fraction is conditional on that sample and is not a total extraction-loss estimate. These results extend the published feed-forward study by identifying particle-response effects relevant to RFKO regulation and tracing transport beyond septum interception. The calculations support configuration-specific interpretations of these mechanisms; they do not quantify the performance of a complete spill-control system.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}

Slow resonant extraction from the Delivery Ring is part of the proton-delivery scheme for the Mu2e experiment at Fermilab~\cite{tdr}. Harmonic sextupoles excite the third-integer resonance, and a ramp of the tune-control quadrupoles moves the beam toward resonance. The preceding Synergia2 study~\cite{mu2e} established a feed-forward approach based on a piecewise linear tune ramp, septum-plane alignment, and dynamic local-orbit bumps. It treated the extraction geometry and average spill evolution, while leaving radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) feedback outside its simulation scope. The present study examines the particle response relevant to RFKO regulation and the subsequent transport of particles scattered at the septum.

The extraction geometry provides the context for both questions. The Hardt condition aligns outgoing separatrices for different momenta and reduces the momentum dependence of the extracted-beam angle~\cite{hardt,pullia,pimms}. In the dispersion-free extraction straight, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} used four time-dependent correctors to control the septum entrance angle. That geometric optimization forms the published baseline for the present particle-response studies.

For its optimized tune ramp, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} reported an open-loop spill within approximately $\pm20\%$ of the mean. RFKO supplies transverse excitation whose amplitude can be adjusted in response to the spill signal~\cite{HB10,ipac11}. Interpreting that response requires distinguishing coherent motion of the bunch from growth of its internal phase-space area, and accounting for the finite time between destabilization and arrival at the septum. We address these aspects through a feedback test under imposed quadrupole ripple, fixed-tune RFKO tests at two chromaticities, and tune-step diagnostics at three stages of extraction.

Septum scattering raises a complementary transport question: where do particles travel or undergo subsequent loss after intercepting extraction material? The published aperture model removes particles classified as septum-head hits or foil-plane crossings~\cite{mu2e}. Here, MARS transports intercepted particles through the extraction channel, and Synergia2 follows a selected returned sample around the ring~\cite{mars1,mars2}. This calculation extends the description beyond geometric interception to the subsequent motion and loss locations of the scattered sample.

The three models serve distinct purposes. Synergia2 provides multiparticle tracking with space charge~\cite{synergia2}; the reduced Kobayashi Hamiltonian isolates horizontal resonance dynamics; and MARS describes interactions with material. Their combined use connects the observed spill and phase-space behavior to particle motion, while the configuration and population boundaries in Sec.~\ref{sec:scope} define the extent of the comparison. The contribution relative to Ref.~\cite{mu2e} is the set of feedback, transit-time, and post-interception diagnostics, building on established RFKO and resonance descriptions.

Section~\ref{sec:scope} relates the calculations to the published baseline and defines the individual simulation cases. Section~\ref{sec:rfko1} presents the feedback response, and Sec.~\ref{sec:tracking} develops the reduced model and examines transit times and scattered-particle transport. Section~\ref{sec:rfko} addresses chromaticity-dependent RFKO excitation, and Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion} brings together the implications for spill regulation and loss interpretation.

The loss populations also differ. \subsection{\label{sec:cases}Simulation cases}

The calculations comprise the five cases in Table~\ref{table:cases}. Each is interpreted individually. Together they provide diagnostics of feedback response, transit dynamics, and scattering transport, not a common, validated model of integrated spill regulation and extraction efficiency. The model comparisons identify qualitative mechanisms without isolating every source of disagreement or establishing numerical controller performance.

Several intervals of about 100 turns occur in this paper and describe different operations. In Ref.~\cite{mu2e}, 100 turns is the sextupole turn-on period and the width of the moving window used to display the spill. In the feedback case, the spill signal is accumulated over approximately 100 turns, about $0.17\,\mathrm{ms}$, and each RFKO chirp sweep lasts 100 turns. Neither interval specifies the controller update interval, which is not documented. Similarly, the 200-turn segments of the published feed-forward ramp are unrelated to the approximately 300-turn pause and 500-turn hold that define the tune-step diagnostic.

\section{\label{sec:rfko1}Spill regulation with RFKO feedback}

The feed-forward result of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} motivates regulating the fluctuations that remain after the average spill has been flattened. The present feedback test adds an imposed quadrupole ripple and a transverse RFKO kick near the horizontal betatron frequency. It examines the beam response to feedback in the stated simulation case, rather than re-establishing the published tune-ramp and dynamic-bump optimization.

The proposed feedback loop comprises an RFKO kicker, a spill monitor, and a controller. A wall-current monitor in the external beamline supplies the extracted-beam signal used to adjust the kicker amplitude~\cite{wcm}. The simulated control scheme uses a spill signal accumulated over approximately 100 turns, corresponding to about $0.17\,\mathrm{ms}$. This averaging interval and the particle transit-time distribution both contribute to the effective response time. Section~\ref{sec:cases} distinguishes it from other intervals of similar length.

The RFKO center frequency follows the betatron frequency associated with the ramped machine tune. Frequency modulation broadens the excitation to cover the chromatic tune spread. In the extraction simulations, the horizontal chromaticity is set to $C_x=d\nu_x/d\delta=1.0$, where $\delta=(p-p_0)/p_0$, using chromaticity-correction sextupoles. The incoherent space-charge tune spread alone need not provide the phase mixing required for efficient emittance growth under dipole excitation~\cite{ipac11}; this distinction is examined further in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}.

The excitation uses a linear frequency chirp with a sweep duration of 100 turns. Repeating an identical sweep produces discrete spectral lines. Randomizing the phase at the start of each sweep reduces this periodic structure and broadens the excitation spectrum.

To test the response to a controlled disturbance, a sinusoidal ripple at $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is applied to the quadrupole strengths. The original simulation description gives its size as a ``$\pm0.5\%$ peak-to-peak'' variation of the nominal strengths, which does not establish whether 0.5\% is the amplitude or the full peak-to-peak excursion. The ripple produces pronounced spill modulation, including components at harmonics of the applied ripple frequency.

Controllers based on proportional--integral--derivative feedback were investigated~\cite{pid}. Proportional-only control produced large spill oscillations, consistent with excessive correction in the presence of a delayed beam response. Among the tested configurations, a time-dependent proportional gain combined with a constant derivative gain gave the most favorable behavior. The result shown here therefore uses proportional--derivative control, without an integral term.

Figure~\ref{fig:fig9} shows a qualitative reduction in ripple-driven modulation with feedback. The remaining spikes indicate that the tested controller does not eliminate all fluctuations. The traces do not establish a numerical suppression factor, a stability margin, or an optimum set of gains. The factor of 1.5--3 ripple reduction discussed in Ref.~\cite{mu2e} was an expectation based on other facilities, not a Mu2e closed-loop result; the present traces do not test that numerical expectation. An integral term could be investigated to reduce steady-state error, but its effect on stability and spill fluctuations has not been demonstrated here.

\section{\label{sec:tracking}Particle tracking simulations}

\subsection{\label{sec:model}Reduced resonance model and Kobayashi Hamiltonian}

Let $x$ be the horizontal displacement from the reference orbit, $x'=dx/ds$ the horizontal angle, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the horizontal Twiss parameters at the observation point. Define the normalized coordinates
\begin{equation}
 X=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 X'=\frac{\alpha x+\beta x'}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 a=\frac{X+iX'}{\sqrt{2}}.
 \label{eqn:coordinates}
\end{equation}
Here $X'$ denotes the normalized momentum coordinate, not $dX/ds$. In the resonance frame, the leading-order third-integer Hamiltonian is~\cite{preliminary}
\begin{equation}
 H=\Delta\nu\,a^*a-ig a^3+ig^*(a^*)^3+\cdots,
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian1}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\nu=\nu_x-29/3$ and $g$ is the complex resonance-driving coefficient determined by the sextupole strengths and phases. The cubic terms are complex conjugates, so their sum is real. Higher-order terms are neglected in the reduced model.

The corresponding geometric Courant--Snyder action is
\begin{equation}
 I_x=a^*a=\frac{X^2+X'^2}{2}
     =\frac{x^2+(\alpha x+\beta x')^2}{2\beta},
 \label{eqn:action}
\end{equation}
consistent with the invariant used for the phase-space aperture in Eq.~(6) of Ref.~\cite{mu2e}. The phase of the resonance coefficient must be compared using the same coordinate convention. With $a=\sqrt{I_x}e^{-i\phi_x}$, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:hamiltonian1} has the angle--action form of Eq.~(1) in Ref.~\cite{mu2e} when its coefficient is written as $g_{\mathrm{aa}}=ig$. This phase factor relates the two Hamiltonian expressions; it does not establish the phase convention used in the original tracking implementation.

Writing $g=|g|e^{i\Psi}$ and substituting Eq.~\eqref{eqn:coordinates} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 H={}&\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}(X^2+X'^2)\\
 &+\frac{|g|}{\sqrt{2}}\left[(X^3-3XX'^2)\sin\Psi
             +(3X^2X'-X'^3)\cos\Psi\right].
\end{split}
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian2}
\end{equation}
For the separatrix orientation used here, set $\Psi=\pi$. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eqn:hamiltonian2} by $6\pi$ gives the three-turn Kobayashi Hamiltonian~\cite{kobayashi},
\begin{equation}
 H_K=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(X^2+X'^2)
       +\frac{S}{4}(3X^2X'-X'^3),
 \label{eqn:kobayashi}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon=6\pi\Delta\nu$ and $S=-12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|$. This sign of $S$ follows from the specified phase $\Psi=\pi$. For the below-resonance setting $\Delta\nu<0$ considered here, it agrees with $12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|\,\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta\nu)$; the latter is not a general definition for a fixed separatrix orientation.

The quadratic term describes linear motion, which is circular in normalized phase space. Below resonance, this motion is counterclockwise with the present coordinate convention. The cubic term creates a triangular stable region bounded by a separatrix, with hyperbolic unstable fixed points at its vertices. Particles outside the stable region can move along the outgoing branches toward the septum.

Introducing the continuous turn variable $\tau=n/3$, where $n$ is the number of revolutions, Hamilton's equations become
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
  \frac{dX}{d\tau} & = \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X^{\prime}}
                  = \epsilon X^{\prime} + \frac{3}{4} S
                    \left(
                      X^{2} - X^{\prime 2}
                    \right), \\
  \frac{dX^{\prime}}{d\tau} & = - \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X}
                           = - \epsilon X - \frac{3}{2} S X X^{\prime}.
 \end{split}
\label{eqn:motion}
\end{equation}
One unit of $\tau$ corresponds to three turns; the physical elapsed time is $t_{\mathrm{phys}}=3T_{\mathrm{rev}}\tau$. The momentum and circumference in Table~\ref{table:param} give $T_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq1.69\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, consistent with $54\,\mathrm{ms}$ over approximately 31,795 turns. The three-turn interval is short compared with the spill duration, but this separation of timescales alone does not guarantee adiabatic motion near a separatrix. The tune steps used below are explicitly nonadiabatic changes followed by tracking at fixed quadrupole strengths.

The reduced model retains horizontal resonance dynamics but omits space charge and detailed lattice and extraction-channel effects. It is used to interpret the Synergia2 trajectories and their transit times. Agreement in qualitative features does not by itself validate the full tracking or scattering calculation.


\subsection{\label{sec:arrival}Transit-time distributions}

The transit time is the interval between a particle becoming unstable and reaching the septum field region~\cite{pullia}. In the diagnostic used here, elapsed time is measured from an imposed tune step. According to the original simulation description, transit times are recorded for the particles in the narrow phase-space shell released by the step. The criterion used to identify these particles is not documented, so particles already in transit before the step may also contribute.

Analytical treatments based on the Kobayashi Hamiltonian describe transit times for fixed or slowly varying resonance conditions~\cite{pullia}. Near an unstable fixed point, the phase-space velocity becomes small and escape can take many turns. This produces a strong dependence on initial conditions and long transit-time tails. Such behavior reflects slow passage near a hyperbolic point, rather than trapping by a stable fixed point.

The Synergia2 calculation starts with $5\times10^5$ macroparticles, the parameters in Table~\ref{table:param}, and a tune-quadrupole ramp described as optimized for a uniform spill. At a selected turn, the ramp is paused for approximately 300 turns, or about 1\% of the spill duration. The strengths are then stepped to the values that the uninterrupted ramp would have reached at that turn and held for a further observation interval of approximately 500 turns. The step reduces the stable region and releases a population near its boundary. Whether the uninterrupted reference ramp is identical to the published 200-turn-segment ramp of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} is not documented. The procedure probes the extraction response to a finite tune change; it is not a measurement of the feedback-loop transfer function.

The pauses begin at turns 2,000, 15,000, and 25,000, representing early, middle, and late extraction. The changing separatrix size and its distance from the septum can affect transit times. Using the same initial macroparticle population at the three stages allows these trends to be compared, although the number of particles contributing to each distribution differs and no statistical-convergence study is presented.

Figure~\ref{fig:arrival1a} shows the evolution of the circulating-beam fraction. The uninterrupted ramp produces a nearly steady decrease in beam intensity. Pausing the ramp slows extraction, and the subsequent tune step produces an additional decrease in circulating intensity. The corresponding normalized spill traces in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival1b} show a pronounced burst in the early-stage case and a weaker, broader response at the later stages. Continued extraction during a pause is consistent with particles already traveling toward the septum and, in the full calculation, with evolving collective dynamics. The traces alone do not isolate the contributions of these effects.

For comparison, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:motion} is integrated with a fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) method. The reduced-model trajectories use the same initial particle coordinates as the Synergia2 sample, and the effective resonance parameters are evaluated at the restored quadrupole settings. Space charge is omitted in the reduced model. The continuous variable $\tau$ must be converted to turns when comparing transit times; observing trajectories every three turns does not prescribe the numerical integration step.

Figure~\ref{fig:arrival2} compares individual-particle transit times and their marginal distributions. Both calculations show short transit times for particles starting close to the septum along the outgoing branch, with increasing times for starting coordinates nearer the unstable fixed point. Away from the short-transit region, the full tracking result has a broader distribution and differs systematically from the reduced model.

Transit time depends on the full initial phase-space coordinates and resonance parameters; it is not generally inversely proportional to the perpendicular distance from a separatrix. Slow escape near a hyperbolic fixed point accounts qualitatively for the long transit times in both calculations. Space charge can change individual trajectories and the effective resonance conditions in Synergia2. However, the comparison also changes the lattice description and other model details, so the observed differences cannot be attributed uniquely to space charge without a matched calculation in which space charge alone is switched off.


\subsection{\label{sec:beamloss}Tracking of particles scattered at the septum}

Particles moving along an outgoing separatrix branch enter the electrostatic septum field and receive a deflection toward the extraction line. The scattering model reported here contains two electrostatic septa with $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ foils. This differs from the $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ geometry in Table~1 and Sec.~5 of Ref.~\cite{mu2e}. Finite foil thickness and extent allow particles to intercept material and scatter. The published geometric hit/cross classification supplies the motivation for following those particles, but its optimized loss fraction is not an input normalization for the present scattering sample.

In the aperture treatment used here, a particle that intercepts a septum component is removed from the primary tracking sample. This does not determine its subsequent fate. A scattered particle may return to the field-free region, receive a partial electrostatic kick, be extracted, or be lost at another aperture. MARS transport is used to resolve these alternatives through the extraction-channel geometry before selected scattered particles are returned to ring tracking.

MARS models hadronic and electromagnetic interactions, including secondary-particle production~\cite{mars2}. Figure~\ref{fig:beamloss1} shows transport through the two electrostatic septa (ESS1 and ESS2), two quadrupoles (Q203 and Q204), and the Lambertson magnet (ELAM). The outgoing beam subsequently reaches the C-magnet. Separation of the circulating and outgoing beams begins at ESS1 and increases before ESS2. The displayed trajectories illustrate this separation but do not, by themselves, establish zero loss at ESS2.

The tracking workflow has three stages. First, a specialized Synergia2 aperture at ESS1 selects particles that hit the foil head or another entrance component, or whose calculated trajectories cross a septum plane inside the element. Second, these particles are transported by MARS from ESS1 through the extraction channel to the C-magnet, using the modeled foil thicknesses, widths, spacings, and materials~\cite{mars1}. Third, the scattered-particle output selected for further ring transport is used as input to Synergia2. The loss fraction below refers to this returned sample; it is not an inclusive count of all secondary species produced by MARS.

The next three turns populate the lower, right, and left regions of the plot. After three turns, many surviving particles have returned to the septum field region. Figure~\ref{fig:beamloss2b} locates losses near the septa, in the first arc, and near the middles of the straight sections. Approximately 43\% of the tracked scattered-particle sample is reported lost, with the remainder surviving to extraction. This conditional fraction cannot be combined with the published geometric loss estimate without matching the foil geometry, sample weights, selection criteria, and tracking endpoints. In particular, it is not evidence of a 43\% loss of the injected beam or of a correction to the published 1.2\% value.

Although the initial scattered-particle distribution is approximately aligned with the septum, its subsequent distributions are curved. After three turns, the added circulating-particle group follows the outgoing separatrix branch, whereas scattered particles can lie away from that branch. The distinction matters when interpreting the apparent shape of the combined distribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:beamloss3} illustrates this deformation using the reduced Hamiltonian. It omits the extraction fields and apertures, so trajectories continue beyond the reference septum line. Particles initially lying on a straight line generally have different Hamiltonian values and follow different level curves at different speeds. Their distribution therefore deforms after three and six turns. This behavior provides a qualitative explanation of phase-space shearing in the Synergia2 results, but it does not validate material scattering, aperture losses, or extraction efficiency.

The tune-quadrupole ramp drives the principal extraction process, while RFKO provides an additional means of regulating the spill. To separate the response to RFKO from the resonance-driven extraction dynamics, this section considers excitation at a fixed machine tune without the third-integer resonance drive. The quantities of interest are emittance, centroid motion, and the distribution of horizontal action.

Earlier simulations found effective excitation when the RFKO center frequency follows the machine tune and the modulation bandwidth is comparable to the chromatic tune spread~\cite{ipac11}. Dipole excitation can drive coherent motion without a corresponding increase in emittance about the beam centroid. A spread in betatron frequencies promotes phase mixing, allowing the excitation to increase the internal phase-space area. An incoherent space-charge tune spread does not necessarily provide the same mixing mechanism as chromaticity.

Unlike the extraction baseline in Ref.~\cite{mu2e}, the horizontal machine tune is fixed at 9.65, and the resonance drive and tune ramp are disabled. The model includes space charge. A circular transverse aperture, specified as a $20\pi\,\mathrm{mm\,mrad}$ acceptance whose normalization is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}, removes particles outside the accepted region. A linear chirp with randomized sweep phases supplies the RFKO excitation; its spectrum is described as covering the betatron tune spread of the beam. Two horizontal chromaticities, $C_x=0$ and $C_x=1$, are compared. The assumed RFKO power is $500\,\mathrm{W}$, corresponding in the modeled kicker to an approximately $1\,\mu\mathrm{rad}$ horizontal kick.

Figure~\ref{fig:rfko1a} shows little emittance growth at $C_x=0$ and substantially greater growth at $C_x=1$. The centroid trajectories in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1b} show the complementary behavior: large coherent excursions at zero chromaticity and motion concentrated near the origin at nonzero chromaticity. These results are consistent with chromatic phase mixing converting the driven response into increased internal beam spread. They do not imply that the RFKO force is absent or decoupled from the particles at zero chromaticity.

Equation~\eqref{eqn:action} defines the underlying geometric action. Quantitative comparison of the plotted action scale with the published normalized acceptance additionally requires the diagnostic units, any relativistic normalization, and the choice of reference orbit or centroid. The action distributions in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko2} remain approximately unchanged for $C_x=0$. At $C_x=1$, the low-action population decreases and the distribution broadens under continued excitation. Because the aperture removes particles, interpreting the distribution quantitatively also requires the surviving population and a fixed normalization convention. These calculations support the role of chromaticity in RFKO-driven emittance growth. Since the resonance drive is disabled in this test, they do not directly demonstrate transfer into an extraction resonance or quantify the resulting spill.


\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusions}

The calculations extend the published feed-forward extraction study for Mu2e~\cite{mu2e} with particle-response diagnostics relevant to RFKO spill regulation and transport beyond septum interception. In the feedback test, proportional--derivative control reduces visible ripple-driven spill modulation, although residual spikes remain. The separate fixed-tune tests show greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions at nonzero chromaticity. Together, these observations support distinguishing the excitation of coherent motion from the phase mixing that can increase the internal beam spread; the fixed-tune tests themselves do not measure extraction.

The tune-step diagnostics show a broad range of arrival times and long delays for particles near unstable fixed points. The reduced Kobayashi model captures the main trends, supporting slow escape near the separatrix as a qualitative explanation of delayed arrival. Its differences from full tracking are not uniquely attributable to space charge because the models also differ in their lattice description and other details. These results show why particle transit times are relevant to interpreting a delayed spill response.

The scattering study follows a selected population beyond the geometric interception model. Approximately 43\% of the sample returned from MARS is reported lost in subsequent ring tracking for the stated $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil configuration. This conditional fraction is distinct from the injected-beam loss quoted for the published $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$-foil model. The existing loss map identifies where the returned particles are lost, while reduced-model trajectories explain their phase-space shearing qualitatively.

The evidence remains specific to the stated cases: it does not provide a quantitative suppression factor, validate an integrated feedback system, or determine total extraction efficiency. Within that scope, the results connect RFKO regulation to two complementary aspects of beam response---chromatic phase mixing and finite transit times---and distinguish primary septum interception from subsequent scattered-particle transport. This separation gives a physical basis for interpreting the spill and loss diagnostics presented here.
