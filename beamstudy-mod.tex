\begin{abstract}
Spill regulation in resonant extraction depends on the beam response to transverse excitation and on the time required for unstable particles to reach the septum. We study these effects for the Fermilab Mu2e Delivery Ring using Synergia2 tracking and a reduced Kobayashi-Hamiltonian model, with MARS transport to examine the subsequent motion of septum-scattered particles. Fixed-tune tests of radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) excitation show greater emittance growth and smaller centroid excursions at nonzero chromaticity, consistent with chromatic phase mixing. In an extraction case with imposed quadrupole ripple, a proportional--derivative RFKO controller reduces the visible ripple-driven spill modulation, with residual spikes. Tune-step diagnostics at three extraction stages show long transit times for particles passing near unstable fixed points; the reduced model reproduces the main trends but differs quantitatively from full tracking. In the stated \SI{50}{\micro\metre}-foil scattering model, approximately 43\% of the scattered-particle sample returned from MARS is lost during subsequent ring tracking; this fraction is conditional on that sample and is not a total extraction-loss estimate. These results extend the published feed-forward study by identifying particle-response effects relevant to RFKO regulation and by following transport beyond septum interception. They are configuration-specific and do not quantify the performance of a complete spill-control system.
\end{abstract}

\section{\label{sec:intro}Introduction}

Slow resonant extraction from the Delivery Ring is part of the proton-delivery scheme for the Mu2e experiment at Fermilab~\cite{tdr}. Harmonic sextupoles excite the third-integer resonance, and a ramp of the tune-control quadrupoles moves the beam toward resonance. The preceding Synergia2 study~\cite{mu2e} established a feed-forward approach based on a piecewise linear tune ramp, septum-plane alignment, and dynamic local-orbit bumps. It treated the extraction geometry and average spill evolution, while leaving radio-frequency knockout (RFKO) feedback outside its simulation scope. This study examines the particle response relevant to RFKO regulation and the subsequent transport of particles scattered at the septum.

The extraction geometry provides the context for both questions. The Hardt condition aligns outgoing separatrices for different momenta and reduces the momentum dependence of the extracted-beam angle~\cite{hardt,pullia,pimms1,pimms2}. In the dispersion-free extraction straight, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} used four time-dependent correctors to control the septum entrance angle. That geometric optimization forms the published baseline for the particle-response studies reported here.

For its optimized tune ramp, Ref.~\cite{mu2e} reported an open-loop spill within approximately $\pm20\%$ of the mean. RFKO supplies transverse excitation whose amplitude can be adjusted in response to the spill signal~\cite{HB10,ipac11}. Interpreting that response requires distinguishing coherent motion of the bunch from growth of its internal phase-space area, and accounting for the finite time between destabilization and arrival at the septum. We address these aspects through fixed-tune RFKO tests at two chromaticities, a feedback test under imposed quadrupole ripple, and tune-step diagnostics at three stages of extraction.

These questions have a substantial background at other slow-extraction facilities. RFKO extraction was proposed as a way to drive particles across a fixed separatrix by transverse excitation~\cite{hiramoto} and was developed at the Heavy Ion Medical Accelerator in Chiba (HIMAC) with amplitude- and frequency-modulated excitation, giving an extraction response within about \SI{1}{ms}~\cite{noda}; feedback on the excitation amplitude has since been used to control the spill~\cite{furukawa}. Recent knock-out studies optimize the excitation signal to minimize spill fluctuations~\cite{niedermayer}, and transit times to the septum have been derived from the Kobayashi Hamiltonian~\cite{pullia}. For Mu2e, the spill-regulation design includes both a tune-quadrupole loop and an RFKO loop~\cite{srs2019}; current simulations implement proportional--integral--derivative (PID) feedback on the fast tune quadrupoles and identify RFKO as a candidate fast regulator still to be compared with it~\cite{srs2025}. The RFKO results reported here bear on that comparison.

Septum scattering raises a complementary transport question: where do particles travel or undergo subsequent loss after intercepting extraction material? Beam loss on the electrostatic septum limits slow-extracted intensity through the activation it causes; crystal shadowing of the septum has been developed and tested with beam at the CERN Super Proton Synchrotron to reduce it~\cite{velotti}, and simulations for Mu2e at \SI{8}{GeV} indicate loss reductions between 40\% and a factor of three~\cite{crystal8gev}. The published aperture model for Mu2e removes particles classified as septum-head hits or foil-plane crossings~\cite{mu2e}. Here, MARS transports intercepted particles through the extraction channel, and Synergia2 follows a selected returned sample around the ring~\cite{mars1,mars2}. This calculation extends the description beyond geometric interception to the subsequent motion and loss locations of the scattered sample.

The three models serve distinct purposes. Synergia2 provides multiparticle tracking with space charge~\cite{synergia2}; the reduced Kobayashi Hamiltonian isolates horizontal resonance dynamics; and MARS describes interactions with material. Their combined use connects the observed spill and phase-space behavior to particle motion, while the configuration and population boundaries in Sec.~\ref{sec:scope} define the extent of the comparison. Relative to Ref.~\cite{mu2e} and to the studies above, the contribution is a set of diagnostics specific to the Mu2e Delivery Ring at high space charge: how the RFKO response differs between zero and nonzero chromaticity, how finite transit times delay the spill response to a tune change, and where septum-scattered particles are lost after interception. The RFKO and resonance descriptions themselves are established.

Section~\ref{sec:scope} relates the calculations to the published baseline and defines the individual simulation cases. Section~\ref{sec:model} presents the reduced resonance model used to interpret the tracking results. Section~\ref{sec:rfkoresp} examines the RFKO response, first the chromatic-mixing mechanism at fixed tune and then feedback during extraction. Sections~\ref{sec:arrival} and~\ref{sec:beamloss} treat transit times after a tune step and the transport of septum-scattered particles. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses how these results combine, and Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the conclusions.

\section{\label{sec:model}Reduced resonance model}

Let $n$ be the turn number and $\theta=2\pi n$ the corresponding orbital angle, both treated as continuous. Let $x$ be the horizontal displacement from the reference orbit, $x'=dx/ds$ its slope with respect to the path length $s$, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the horizontal Twiss parameters at the observation point. The normalized coordinates are
\begin{equation}
 X=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 X'=\frac{\alpha x+\beta x'}{\sqrt{\beta}},\qquad
 a=\frac{X+iX'}{\sqrt{2}}.
 \label{eqn:coordinates}
\end{equation}
Here $X'$ is the momentum conjugate to $X$, not $dX/ds$. When $x$ and $\beta$ are expressed in consistent length units, $X$ and $X'$ have units of $\sqrt{\mathrm{length}}$. The geometric Courant--Snyder action,
\begin{equation}
 I_x=a^*a=\frac{X^2+X'^2}{2}
     =\frac{x^2+(\alpha x+\beta x')^2}{2\beta},
 \label{eqn:action}
\end{equation}
has units of length, for example $\mathrm{mm\,mrad}$, and is consistent with the invariant used for the phase-space aperture in Eq.~(6) of Ref.~\cite{mu2e}.

Action and emittance are related but distinct. For a matched distribution centered on the reference orbit, the rms geometric emittance equals the mean action $\langle I_x\rangle$. The normalized emittance is larger by the relativistic factor $p/(m_pc)\simeq9.47$ for the momentum in Table~\ref{table:param}. For a Gaussian distribution, 95\% of the particles satisfy $2I_x\le6\langle I_x\rangle$, which is a common basis for quoting a 95\% emittance. If the centroid oscillates, an emittance computed about the centroid excludes the coherent motion, whereas the mean action about the reference orbit includes it. This distinction underlies the comparison of emittance growth and centroid motion in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}.

In a frame rotating with the resonant tune $29/3$, so that a particle exactly on resonance is stationary when observed once per turn, and with $\theta$ as the independent variable, the leading-order third-integer Hamiltonian is~\cite{preliminary}
\begin{equation}
 H=\Delta\nu\,a^*a-ig a^3+ig^*(a^*)^3+\cdots.
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian1}
\end{equation}
Here $\nu_x$ is the horizontal betatron tune, $\Delta\nu=\nu_x-29/3$ is its distance from resonance, and $g$ is the complex resonance-driving coefficient, with units of $\mathrm{length}^{-1/2}$, determined by the harmonic-sextupole strengths and phases. The two cubic terms are complex conjugates, so $H$ is real; higher-order terms are neglected. The equations of motion are $da/d\theta=-i\,\partial H/\partial a^*$, equivalent to $dX/d\theta=\partial H/\partial X'$ and $dX'/d\theta=-\partial H/\partial X$.

The phase of the resonance coefficient must be compared using the same coordinate convention. With $a=\sqrt{I_x}e^{-i\phi_x}$, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:hamiltonian1} has the angle--action form of Eq.~(1) in Ref.~\cite{mu2e} when its coefficient is written as $g_{\mathrm{aa}}=ig$. This phase factor relates the two Hamiltonian expressions; it does not establish the phase convention used in the original tracking implementation.

Writing $g=|g|e^{i\Psi}$ and substituting Eq.~\eqref{eqn:coordinates} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 H={}&\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}(X^2+X'^2)\\
 &+\frac{|g|}{\sqrt{2}}\left[(X^3-3XX'^2)\sin\Psi
             +(3X^2X'-X'^3)\cos\Psi\right].
\end{split}
 \label{eqn:hamiltonian2}
\end{equation}
The phase $\Psi$ sets the orientation of the separatrix; we use $\Psi=\pi$, for which the $\sin\Psi$ term vanishes. Measuring time in units of three turns with $\tau=n/3=\theta/(6\pi)$ multiplies $H$ by $6\pi$ and gives the Kobayashi Hamiltonian~\cite{kobayashi},
\begin{equation}
 H_K=\frac{\epsilon}{2}(X^2+X'^2)
       +\frac{S}{4}(3X^2X'-X'^3),
 \label{eqn:kobayashi}
\end{equation}
with $\epsilon=6\pi\Delta\nu$ and $S=-12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|$. The symbol $\epsilon$ follows Kobayashi's notation for the tune distance and is not an emittance. The negative sign of $S$ follows from $\Psi=\pi$. For the below-resonance setting $\Delta\nu<0$ considered here, it agrees with the expression $12\sqrt{2}\pi|g|\,\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta\nu)$, which does not define a fixed separatrix orientation in general.

The quadratic term describes rotation in normalized phase space, counterclockwise for $\Delta\nu<0$ in the present convention. The cubic term bounds a triangular stable region around the stable fixed point at the origin. Below resonance, $\epsilon/S=|\Delta\nu|/(2\sqrt{2}|g|)>0$, and the three unstable fixed points are
\begin{equation}
 (X,X')=\left(0,\ \frac{4\epsilon}{3S}\right),\qquad
 \left(\pm\frac{2\epsilon}{\sqrt{3}\,S},\ -\frac{2\epsilon}{3S}\right),
 \label{eqn:fixedpoints}
\end{equation}
all at distance $4\epsilon/(3S)$ from the origin. The separatrix consists of the three straight lines $X'=-2\epsilon/(3S)$ and $X'=4\epsilon/(3S)\pm\sqrt{3}X$, which join these hyperbolic points. Their extensions beyond the vertices are the branches along which unstable particles leave the stable region and move toward the septum. The linear size of the stable region is proportional to $|\Delta\nu|$, so the region shrinks as the tune approaches resonance. The leftmost unstable fixed point, marked in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival2}, has $X=-2\epsilon/(\sqrt{3}S)$.

In terms of $\tau$, Hamilton's equations are
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
  \frac{dX}{d\tau} & = \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X^{\prime}}
                  = \epsilon X^{\prime} + \frac{3}{4} S
                    \left(
                      X^{2} - X^{\prime 2}
                    \right), \\
  \frac{dX^{\prime}}{d\tau} & = - \frac{\partial H_{K}}{\partial X}
                           = - \epsilon X - \frac{3}{2} S X X^{\prime}.
 \end{split}
\label{eqn:motion}
\end{equation}
One unit of $\tau$ corresponds to three turns, so the elapsed time is $t=nT_{\mathrm{rev}}=3T_{\mathrm{rev}}\tau$. The momentum and circumference in Table~\ref{table:param} give $T_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq\SI{1.69}{\micro\second}$, consistent with \SI{54}{\milli\second} over approximately 31,795 turns. The three-turn interval is short compared with the spill duration, but this separation of timescales alone does not guarantee adiabatic motion near a separatrix. The tune steps used below are explicitly nonadiabatic changes followed by tracking at fixed quadrupole strengths.

The reduced model retains horizontal resonance dynamics but omits space charge and detailed lattice and extraction-channel effects. It is used to interpret the Synergia2 transit times (Sec.~\ref{sec:arrival}) and the scattered-particle distributions (Sec.~\ref{sec:beamloss}).

\section{\label{sec:rfkoresp}RFKO response: chromatic mixing and spill feedback}

RFKO regulates the spill by changing the transverse amplitudes of particles near the stable-region boundary, while the tune-quadrupole ramp drives the principal extraction process. This section first isolates how the excitation changes the beam distribution, using fixed-tune tests at two chromaticities (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}), and then examines feedback during extraction with an imposed quadrupole ripple (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}).

\subsection{\label{sec:rfko}Fixed-tune excitation: emittance and centroid motion}

To isolate how RFKO changes the beam distribution, this subsection considers excitation at a fixed machine tune without the third-integer resonance drive. The diagnostics are emittance, centroid motion, and the distribution of horizontal action.

A transverse dipole kick deflects every particle in the bunch by the same angle. If all particles had the same betatron tune, successive kicks would change only the coherent oscillation of the centroid, leaving the distribution about the centroid, and hence the emittance about the centroid, unchanged. A spread of betatron tunes makes the particles dephase after each kick, so the coherent oscillation filaments into a larger internal phase-space area: the centroid motion decays while the emittance grows. Chromaticity adds a chromatic tune spread $C_x\delta$, of order $10^{-3}$ for $C_x=1$ and the momentum spread in Table~\ref{table:param}. Earlier simulations for this extraction found effective excitation when the RFKO center frequency follows the machine tune and the modulation bandwidth is comparable to the chromatic tune spread~\cite{ipac11}. They also found that the incoherent space-charge tune spread alone does not provide this mixing: the coherent betatron oscillation of the bunch as a whole is not affected by the internal space-charge forces, so at zero chromaticity the dipole oscillation does not decohere, and chromaticity must be added to dilute it~\cite{ipac11}. Physically, a rigidly displaced bunch carries its direct space-charge field with it.

Unlike the extraction baseline in Ref.~\cite{mu2e}, the horizontal machine tune is fixed at 9.65, and the resonance drive and tune ramp are disabled. The model includes space charge. A circular transverse aperture, specified as a $20\pi\,\mathrm{mm\,mrad}$ acceptance whose normalization is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}, removes particles outside the accepted region. A linear chirp with randomized sweep phases supplies the RFKO excitation; its spectrum is described as covering the betatron tune spread of the beam. Two horizontal chromaticities, $C_x=0$ and $C_x=1$, are compared. The chirp is described as covering the betatron tune spread. At $C_x=0$ the chromatic contribution to that spread vanishes while the space-charge spread remains, so the two cases may differ in excitation spectrum as well as in chromaticity; the numerical bandwidths are not documented. The assumed RFKO power is \SI{500}{\watt}, corresponding in the modeled kicker to an approximately \SI{1}{\micro\radian} horizontal kick.

Figure~\ref{fig:rfko1} shows the expected contrast. At $C_x=0$ the emittance stays nearly constant (Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1a}), while the centroid wanders irregularly with excursions of several millimeters and milliradians (Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1b}). This is the behavior expected when the coherent oscillation does not decohere: without chromatic spread, the dipole mode is unaffected by the incoherent space-charge spread~\cite{ipac11}, so each randomized sweep adds a response of random phase to the coherent oscillation, whose amplitude therefore wanders rather than growing steadily. At $C_x=1$ the centroid remains near the origin while the emittance grows substantially, consistent with chromatic phase mixing converting the driven motion into internal spread. Because the two cases may also differ in excitation bandwidth, the comparison is consistent with this mechanism but does not by itself isolate chromaticity as its cause. The growth slows during the later part of the run; because particles outside the aperture are removed, this slowing may partly reflect particle removal rather than a change in the excitation. The small emittance change at $C_x=0$ does not mean that the RFKO force is absent: the excitation acts on every particle but, without chromatic decoherence, mainly moves the bunch as a whole.

Figure~\ref{fig:rfko2} shows the diagnostic $f(\sqrt{I_x})/\sqrt{I_x}$ of the original plots. Its relation to Eq.~\eqref{eqn:action} is not established, because the diagnostic does not document whether the action is measured about the reference orbit or about the centroid. The distinction matters at $C_x=0$: about the reference orbit, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:action} gives $\langle I_x\rangle=[\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(X')+\langle X\rangle^2+\langle X'\rangle^2]/2$, so the coherent displacement seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1b} would contribute even with unchanged internal emittance. If $I_x$ is measured about a fixed reference and $f(u)=dN/du$ with $u=\sqrt{I_x}$, the annulus between $u$ and $u+du$ in the $(X,X')$ plane has area $4\pi u\,du$, so $f(u)/u$ is proportional to the phase-space density averaged over angle at radius $\sqrt{2}\,u$. For a centered, matched Gaussian beam in these coordinates, it is a Gaussian function of $u$; a displaced or mismatched beam need not give that profile. With these caveats, the $C_x=0$ profiles at all times nearly coincide, consistent with the nearly constant emittance in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1a}. That coincidence despite the centroid excursions in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfko1b} could reflect a centroid-referenced diagnostic, excursions small compared with the beam size in the diagnostic's units, or sampling times that differ between the figures; the available records do not distinguish these. At $C_x=1$ the central value falls steadily while the profile flattens and extends beyond the plotted range. This redistribution toward larger amplitudes is consistent with amplitude growth driven by the excitation, but the profiles do not establish the history of individual particles. Because the aperture removes particles, the population is not conserved, and the profiles are compared only qualitatively.

For spill regulation, the relevant effect is the growth of individual amplitudes near the stable-region boundary, which governs how quickly particles cross the separatrix. The fixed-tune results are consistent with nonzero chromaticity converting RFKO excitation into such growth, as the earlier simulations found~\cite{ipac11}, whereas at zero chromaticity the excitation mainly drives the bunch as a whole; the undocumented difference in bandwidth between the two cases prevents attributing the contrast to chromaticity alone. This supports the use of $C_x=1$ in the feedback case that follows (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}). Because the resonance drive is disabled in this test, the results do not show how the increased spread translates into an extraction rate or quantify the resulting spill.

\subsection{\label{sec:rfko1}Spill regulation with feedback under quadrupole ripple}

The feed-forward result of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} motivates regulating the fluctuations that remain after the average spill has been flattened. The feedback test adds an imposed quadrupole ripple and a transverse RFKO kick near the horizontal betatron frequency.

Feedback on the RFKO amplitude has been used to control the spill in medical synchrotrons~\cite{furukawa}. The proposed Mu2e loop comprises an RFKO kicker, a spill monitor, and a controller. A wall-current monitor in the external beamline supplies the extracted-beam signal used to adjust the kicker amplitude~\cite{wcm,rfkoreg}. The simulated control scheme uses a spill signal accumulated over approximately 100 turns, corresponding to about \SI{0.17}{\milli\second}. This averaging interval and the particle transit-time distribution both contribute to the effective response time. Section~\ref{sec:cases} distinguishes it from other intervals of similar length.

The RFKO center frequency follows the betatron frequency associated with the ramped machine tune. Frequency modulation broadens the excitation to cover the chromatic tune spread. In the extraction simulations, the horizontal chromaticity is set to $C_x=d\nu_x/d\delta=1.0$, where $\delta=(p-p_0)/p_0$, using chromaticity-correction sextupoles. The fixed-tune response in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko} is consistent with this chromatic spread providing the phase mixing needed for emittance growth under dipole excitation, as earlier simulations showed~\cite{ipac11}.

The excitation uses a linear frequency chirp with a sweep duration of 100 turns. Repeating an identical sweep produces discrete spectral lines. Randomizing the phase at the start of each sweep reduces this periodic structure and broadens the excitation spectrum.

To test the response to a controlled disturbance, a ripple at \SI{60}{\hertz} is applied to the quadrupole strengths. The original simulation description gives its size as a ``$\pm0.5\%$ peak-to-peak'' variation of the nominal strengths, which does not establish whether 0.5\% is the amplitude or the full peak-to-peak excursion; the waveform is not specified. The ripple produces pronounced, quasi-periodic spill modulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig9}, middle). Its dominant visible modulation repeats several times within one \SI{60}{\hertz} period, which corresponds to about 9,830 turns, so the relation between the applied waveform and the spill spectrum is not established by the displayed traces.

Controllers based on proportional--integral--derivative feedback were investigated~\cite{pid}. Proportional-only control produced large spill oscillations (not shown), consistent with overcorrection when the beam responds to the kicker with a delay. In the original study, a proportional gain that varied during the spill, combined with a constant derivative gain, performed best among the tested configurations; the comparison criterion and gain values are not documented. The result shown here therefore uses proportional--derivative control, without an integral term.

Figure~\ref{fig:fig9} compares the three configurations. Each trace begins with a brief start-up transient. Without ripple or feedback (top), the normalized spill fluctuates about unity with small, fast variations. The imposed ripple (middle) adds larger, quasi-periodic excursions with a sawtooth-like shape. With feedback (bottom), this modulation is visibly smaller, although still discernible, and isolated spikes appear; the largest of these slightly exceed the largest excursions without feedback.

Both features are consistent with a loop whose corrections reach the spill only after a delay. The controller acts on a spill averaged over about 100 turns, and particles moved toward the separatrix by the kicker reach the septum only after transit times that range from a few turns to more than one hundred turns in the tune-step diagnostic (Sec.~\ref{sec:arrival}). A correction therefore responds to spill changes that have already occurred: slow modulation can be reduced, whereas abrupt changes can be under- or overcorrected. The derivative term, which responds to the rate of change of the spill, is a standard means of damping the oscillation seen with proportional-only control~\cite{pid}.

The result shows that, in this space-charge tracking model, an RFKO loop acting on a spill averaged over about 100 turns reduces the visible effect of an imposed quadrupole ripple. An integral term could reduce steady-state error; its effect was not examined.

\section{\label{sec:arrival}Transit times after a tune step}

The transit time of a particle is the interval between its becoming unstable and its reaching the septum field region~\cite{pullia}. This single-particle quantity has no common time origin. The diagnostic used here instead measures, for each selected particle, the elapsed time from a common tune step, namely the restoration of the quadrupole strengths described below, until the particle reaches the septum field region. According to the original simulation description, transit times are recorded for the particles in the narrow phase-space shell released by the step. For these particles the two definitions nearly coincide, because the step makes them unstable. The selection criterion and the numerical arrival criterion are not documented, so particles already in transit before the step may also contribute, with elapsed times shorter than their full transit times.

Analytical treatments based on the Kobayashi Hamiltonian describe transit times for fixed or slowly varying resonance conditions~\cite{pullia}. Near an unstable fixed point, the phase-space velocity becomes small and escape can take many turns. This produces a strong dependence on initial conditions and long transit-time tails. Such behavior reflects slow passage near a hyperbolic point, located by Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fixedpoints}, rather than trapping by a stable fixed point.

The Synergia2 calculation starts with $5\times10^5$ macroparticles, the parameters in Table~\ref{table:param}, and a tune-quadrupole ramp described as optimized for a uniform spill. At a selected turn, the ramp is paused for approximately 300 turns, or about 1\% of the spill duration. The strengths are then stepped to the values that the uninterrupted ramp would have reached at that turn and held for a further observation interval of approximately 500 turns. The step reduces the stable region and releases a population near its boundary. The procedure probes the extraction response to a finite tune change.

The pauses begin at turns 2,000, 15,000, and 25,000, representing early, middle, and late extraction. As the ramp proceeds, the stable region shrinks, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:fixedpoints}, and the distance from the unstable fixed points to the septum grows. The three stages share one simulated beam, which allows these trends to be compared, although the number of particles contributing to each distribution differs.

Figure~\ref{fig:arrival1a} shows the evolution of the circulating-beam fraction. The uninterrupted ramp produces a nearly steady decrease in beam intensity. Pausing the ramp slows extraction, and the subsequent tune step produces an additional decrease in circulating intensity. The corresponding normalized spill traces in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival1b} show a pronounced burst in the early-stage case and a weaker, broader response at the later stages. The burst size follows how strongly the pause suppresses extraction: at the early stage the spill nearly stops during the pause, so the step releases a larger accumulated population, whereas at the later stages extraction continues at a reduced rate. The original simulation description attributes this difference to a thicker phase-space shell released at the early stage. In each case the burst follows the restoration line after a visible delay and spreads over a finite interval, as expected from the distribution of transit times of the released particles in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival2}. After the burst, the paused-and-restored case extracts more slowly than the uninterrupted ramp for the rest of the displayed interval: its spill stays below, and its circulating fraction above, the corresponding blue trace. This difference is expected while the quadrupole strengths are held fixed and the uninterrupted ramp continues. The displayed interval extends beyond the approximately 500-turn hold, and the ramp protocol after the hold is not documented. Continued extraction during a pause is consistent with particles already traveling toward the septum and, in the full calculation, with evolving collective dynamics; the traces do not separate these contributions.

For comparison, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:motion} is integrated with a fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) method. Figure~\ref{fig:arrival2} compares individual-particle transit times and their marginal distributions. Both calculations give short and nearly identical transit times for particles starting between the septum and the fixed-point line, which are already on the outgoing branch. Transit times increase as the starting coordinate moves from the septum toward $x=0$. The longest times belong to particles starting on the far side of the fixed-point line; in the reduced model, such particles must travel along the separatrix past an unstable fixed point before reaching the outgoing branch. Away from the short-transit region, the Synergia2 times scatter more widely at a given starting coordinate and are generally longer than the reduced-model times. At the middle and late stages, however, the reduced-model points reach the longest times shown, for particles starting nearest $x=0$. The two calculations therefore differ systematically, but neither has uniformly the broader distribution. In the short-transit region, the transit times of both calculations fall on discrete levels, as expected because a particle on an outgoing branch returns to the septum side of phase space only once every three turns. From the early to the late stage, the fixed-point line moves toward $x=0$ while the septum line stays fixed, the starting coordinates concentrate closer to $x=0$, and the longest transit times shown are longer at the middle and late stages than at the early stage.

Transit time depends on the full initial phase-space coordinates and the resonance parameters, and in particular on how closely a trajectory passes an unstable fixed point, where the phase-space velocity vanishes. This slow passage accounts qualitatively for the long transit times in both calculations and for their increase at later stages, when the stable region is smaller and its unstable fixed points lie farther from the septum. Space charge, present only in Synergia2, is one possible source of the differences between the two calculations (Sec.~\ref{sec:modeldisc}). Because the hold lasts about 500 turns and the plotted range ends at 250 turns, the displayed distributions may also omit the longest delays.

For spill regulation, the relevant feature is the spread of transit times: particles released at the same moment reach the septum over an interval ranging from a few turns to more than one hundred turns, and this interval lengthens at later stages. A change in tune or in RFKO amplitude therefore alters the spill gradually rather than at once, which contributes to the delayed response discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}.

\section{\label{sec:beamloss}Transport of septum-scattered particles}

Particles moving along an outgoing separatrix branch enter the electrostatic septum field and receive a deflection toward the extraction line. Because the septum foils have finite thickness and extent, some particles intercept material and scatter; this interception is a principal source of beam loss and activation in slow extraction~\cite{velotti}. The scattering model reported here contains two electrostatic septa with \SI{50}{\micro\metre} foils, whereas the extraction model of Ref.~\cite{mu2e} uses \SI{100}{\micro\metre} foils (Table~\ref{table:scope}). That study removes intercepted particles through a geometric hit/cross classification; its loss fraction motivates following those particles but does not normalize the present sample.

Removal from the primary tracking sample does not determine the fate of a scattered particle. Scattering changes the particle's angle and hence its action, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:action}. The particle may then return to the field-free region and continue circulating, receive a partial electrostatic kick, be extracted, or be lost at another aperture. The calculation below follows these alternatives through the extraction channel and around the ring.

The calculation has three stages, each addressing a different question.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Primary interception (Synergia2).} A specialized aperture at the entrance of the first electrostatic septum (ESS1) selects particles that hit the foil head or another entrance component, or whose trajectories cross a septum plane inside the element. These particles are removed from the primary extraction calculation.
  \item \emph{Material transport (MARS).} The intercepted particles are transported from ESS1 through the extraction channel to the C-magnet downstream of the Lambertson magnet, using the modeled foil thicknesses, widths, spacings, and materials~\cite{mars1}. MARS models hadronic and electromagnetic interactions, including secondary-particle production~\cite{mars2}.
  \item \emph{Ring tracking (Synergia2).} The part of the MARS output selected for further ring transport, recorded at the C-magnet, is tracked around the Delivery Ring until each particle is lost or survives.
\end{enumerate}
The loss fraction below refers to the sample returned in stage~3. It is not an inclusive count of all secondary species produced by MARS.

\section{\label{sec:discussion}Discussion}

\subsection{\label{sec:chain}Response chain for RFKO spill regulation}

The results can be read as successive links between a change in kicker amplitude and a change in the measured spill. First, the excitation must increase the amplitudes of individual particles rather than only drive the bunch as a whole; the fixed-tune tests are consistent with nonzero chromaticity providing this conversion, as earlier simulations found~\cite{ipac11}, although a difference in excitation bandwidth between the two cases is not excluded (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}, Figs.~\ref{fig:rfko1} and~\ref{fig:rfko2}). Second, particles carried across the separatrix reach the septum over a spread of times, from a few turns to more than one hundred turns in the tune-step diagnostic, with longer delays at later stages (Sec.~\ref{sec:arrival}, Fig.~\ref{fig:arrival2}). Third, the controller acts on a spill signal averaged over about 100 turns (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko1}). The transit-time spread is therefore comparable to, and can exceed, the averaging interval, so a correction acts on spill changes that have already occurred. This explains qualitatively why proportional-only control produced large oscillations and why a derivative term helped, and it is consistent with the residual spikes in Fig.~\ref{fig:fig9}.

Because the transit-time spread grows as extraction proceeds, the effective delay of the loop is likely to change during the spill. The tested controller used a proportional gain that varied during the spill; whether its schedule was chosen to compensate for such a change is not documented, and the present results do not determine a gain schedule. None of the links is quantified here as a transfer function: the fixed-tune tests disable the resonance drive, the tune-step diagnostic uses a finite step and a specific particle selection, and the feedback traces provide no suppression metric. Current Mu2e regulation studies plan to compare RFKO with PID regulation by the fast tune quadrupoles~\cite{srs2025}; chromatic mixing and the transit-time spread are two properties that such a comparison has to account for, since a tune change also acts only through the transit of released particles to the septum.

\subsection{\label{sec:lossdisc}Losses after septum interception}

The scattering calculation changes how losses near the extraction region should be interpreted. Crystal shadowing reduces the number of particles that reach the septum~\cite{velotti,crystal8gev}; the present calculation addresses the complementary question of what happens to particles that are intercepted. A geometric aperture model counts an intercepted particle as lost at the septum. In the returned sample, however, scattered particles continue around the ring, return to the septum region every third turn, and are lost near the septa, in the first arc downstream of extraction, and at other locations (Sec.~\ref{sec:beamloss}, Fig.~\ref{fig:beamloss2}). Part of the loss associated with septum interception therefore appears elsewhere in the ring, and surviving particles have repeated opportunities to be extracted. The approximately 43\% fraction applies only to the returned sample, so the size of this redistribution for the circulating beam is not determined.

\subsection{\label{sec:modeldisc}Role of the reduced model}

The reduced Kobayashi model provides a common interpretation for two otherwise different results. In the tune-step diagnostic it explains why particles that pass close to an unstable fixed point take longest to reach the septum (Sec.~\ref{sec:arrival}); in the scattering study it explains why a line of scattered particles shears as particles on different level curves move at different speeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:beamloss}). In both cases the full Synergia2 tracking differs quantitatively from the reduced model. The two calculations differ in space charge, lattice description, and the treatment of the extraction channel, and no matched calculation isolates one of these, so the differences cannot be assigned to a single cause. The reduced model therefore identifies mechanisms rather than predicting transit-time distributions or loss fractions, and qualitative agreement does not validate the full tracking or scattering calculations.

\subsection{\label{sec:limits}Scope of the evidence}

The five cases are interpreted individually and do not form a common, validated model of integrated spill regulation and extraction efficiency; which published settings each case inherited, and the numerical convergence of its observables, are not established (Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}). The fixed-tune comparison of chromaticities does not exclude a difference in excitation bandwidth, and the reference of its action diagnostic is not documented (Sec.~\ref{sec:rfko}). The feedback result is qualitative: the traces give no suppression factor, stability margin, or optimum gains, and they do not test the factor of 1.5--3 ripple reduction that Ref.~\cite{mu2e} expected from experience at other facilities. Several conventions of the original plots are not documented---the spill normalization, the emittance and action definitions, the normalization of the transit-time coordinate, the loss-map legend, and the phase-space overlays---so these figures are interpreted qualitatively, and their captions identify each case. The approximately 43\% loss is conditional on the returned scattered-particle sample (Sec.~\ref{sec:baseline}).

\section{\label{sec:conclusion}Conclusions}

The calculations extend the published feed-forward extraction study for Mu2e~\cite{mu2e} with particle-response diagnostics relevant to RFKO spill regulation and with the transport of septum-scattered particles beyond geometric interception.

At fixed tune, RFKO excitation produced emittance growth with nonzero chromaticity and mainly coherent centroid motion at zero chromaticity, consistent with chromatic phase mixing; the comparison does not exclude a contribution from an undocumented difference in excitation bandwidth. This supports the use of nonzero chromaticity for RFKO regulation, although the fixed-tune tests do not measure extraction. In an extraction case with imposed quadrupole ripple, proportional--derivative RFKO feedback reduces the visible ripple-driven spill modulation, with residual spikes.

After a tune step, particles reach the septum over a broad spread of times, with the longest delays for particles passing near unstable fixed points and longer delays later in the spill. The reduced Kobayashi model reproduces these trends qualitatively.

In the stated \SI{50}{\micro\metre}-foil scattering model, approximately 43\% of the scattered-particle sample returned from MARS is reported lost in subsequent ring tracking, near the septa and elsewhere around the ring. This fraction is conditional on the returned sample and is distinct from the injected-beam loss quoted for the published \SI{100}{\micro\metre}-foil model.

Taken together (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}), chromatic mixing and finite transit times form successive links between a change in RFKO amplitude and the measured spill, and septum interception is followed by transport that moves part of the associated loss around the ring. These conclusions are specific to the stated cases and do not quantify the performance of an integrated spill-control system or the total extraction efficiency (Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}).
